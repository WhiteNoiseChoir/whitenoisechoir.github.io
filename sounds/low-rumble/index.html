Source-specific listening guide

A very low weighted sound—not a test signal

Low Rumble is an independently rendered 600-second filtered random texture. It is deliberately bass-concentrated but is not an engine recording, seismic recording, subwoofer calibration track or single-frequency tone. The generator shapes a sloping random spectrum with a low-pass cutoff near 280 Hz and a low-frequency high-pass transition. Cutoff values are source-design settings; the published decoded MP3 measurements below are the evidence for how the final file actually distributes energy.

The five measured sample bands drop sharply above the first 80–250 Hz region. The 750–2,000 Hz band is much weaker, and the upper bands contain very little relative energy. That sharp reduction is reflected in a fitted −43.91 dB-per-decade slope over the reported 120–6,000 Hz range. It is a property of this processed master, not a textbook universal definition of low rumble.

Small mobile speakers often attenuate bass, so the sound can feel unusually faint on a phone even when the audio is playing correctly. Avoid raising the volume aggressively to compensate; test at a comfortable level with equipment that reproduces low frequencies well. The numeric digital loudness shown in the facts table cannot tell you the sound-pressure level delivered by headphones or speakers.

The nearest listening comparisons are Brown Noise and Deep Brown Noise. They are distinct generated files with different low-frequency shaping, but the audience seeking soft bass-heavy background sound may find overlapping uses. Airplane Cabin is more conceptually machine-like because it has a generated tonal component. Low Rumble remains a descriptive texture rather than a standard acoustic color.

When a bass-heavy bed is useful

Some people prefer a low, unobtrusive layer during reading or relaxation. This page allows listening and comparison without asserting that bass solves insomnia, masks every disruptive sound or induces a therapeutic response. High-frequency interruptions may remain very audible above a low-frequency sound bed.

Rumble versus Brown Noise

Brown Noise is usually discussed through a nominal decreasing power spectrum; Low Rumble is an intentionally bandwidth-limited variation. The difference is not simply that one slider is louder. Use the shared player to audition the actual tracks at sensible volume; do not equate similar labels with identical frequency response.

Not suitable for equipment evaluation

Do not use this recording to diagnose speaker bass extension, room resonance, headphone safety or audio faults. Those applications require calibrated signals, measurement conditions and proper instrumentation that this entertainment-oriented file does not provide.

The measurements come from this site's source-generation and QA code, with track-level values preserved in the DSP manifest. The figures help compare frequency emphasis; they do not substitute for a calibrated listening experiment or actual field recording.

Choosing a listening situation

Use this source for personal comparisons of continuous low backgrounds, beginning at comfortable volume. Bass-heavy sound can be less audible on small speakers; boosting volume indiscriminately is not a safe workaround.

Questions about Low Rumble

Is Low Rumble the same as a subwoofer test?

No. It is a listening texture, not a calibrated single-frequency test tone or speaker-performance measurement.

Why do I hear less rumble through an iPhone speaker?

Small speakers often attenuate low frequencies. That is a device limitation rather than proof the file contains no energy.

Does the sound stop after ten minutes?

No. Ten minutes describes the MP3 master; the player uses continuous HLS or MP3 looping until stopped, the timer expires, or your device interrupts playback. HLS repeats the master rather than creating additional independent audio.

Is Low Rumble the same as Brown Noise?

No. Both emphasize lower frequencies, but they are independently synthesized masters with different spectral filters. They may still compete for similar listening intents.

Should I raise the volume if the rumble is faint on my phone?

Not necessarily. Many phone speakers cannot reproduce very low energy efficiently. Raising volume is not a reliable or hearing-safe way to restore missing bass.
