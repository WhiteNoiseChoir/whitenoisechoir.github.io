Understanding Warm Pink Noise

What “warm” means for this file

Warm Pink Noise is a descriptive label for a custom 600-second recording, not a new conventional acoustic color. Ideal Pink Noise follows a power-density relationship close to 1/f, often described as −10 dB per decade. Our Warm Pink audio measures about −41.65 dB per decade over the analyzed range because additional shaping, including a cutoff around 2,000 Hz, strongly reduces much of the higher end.

It can therefore sound noticeably darker than standard Pink Noise, even though the word “pink” is still in its title. For textbook spectral comparison or audio measurement, use the original Pink Noise reference instead. This warmer variant is offered as a listening option with its actual measured behavior disclosed, not as a scientific calibration file.

Warm Pink versus Brown and Deep Brown

Although its upper range is filtered, Warm Pink still has a different measured profile from Brown Noise and the especially deep Deep Brown Noise variant. A darker tonal curve may be pleasant for some listeners, but the perceived result changes across earbuds, laptop speakers and full-range monitors. Compare at low volume and choose by sound, not by a claim of therapeutic benefit.

For conventional spectral definitions, see IEEE's colored-noise overview and the American Academy of Audiology's listening-volume caution. These are references for the physical principles, not endorsements of the recording.

Choosing a listening situation

Try it when standard Pink Noise feels too bright, comparing at a comfortable level. A low-heavy spectrum can reproduce differently on earbuds, laptop speakers and a larger speaker.

Questions about Warm Pink Noise

Is Warm Pink Noise the same thing as standard Pink Noise?

No. Its measured spectral tilt differs substantially from the conventional 1/f curve; it is a custom tonal variation.

Can I compare them using the same device volume?

You can, but the tracks are not automatically loudness-matched and equal percentage settings do not guarantee equal perceived levels.

Does the sound stop after ten minutes?

No. Ten minutes describes the MP3 master; the player uses continuous HLS or MP3 looping until stopped, the timer expires, or your device interrupts playback. HLS repeats the master rather than creating additional independent audio.

Does Warm Pink Noise follow the usual 1/f rule?

Not across the whole tested band. Its measured fitted tilt is −41.65 dB per decade after additional filtering, considerably steeper than ideal Pink Noise's −10 dB per decade.

How is Warm Pink different from simply lowering playback volume?

Frequency shaping changes the balance of highs and lows, whereas lowering a volume slider reduces the overall signal without changing its spectral distribution.
