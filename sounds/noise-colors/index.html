About this category

White noise distributes power broadly across frequencies; pink noise emphasizes lower bands relative to white, while brown noise places even more energy toward bass frequencies. Blue and violet weight the treble instead. Green Noise has no agreed spectral standard; our separately generated version concentrates energy in a measured middle band using disclosed filters.

Choosing your next sound

Which noise color feels most comfortable depends on the listener and their playback equipment. Use similar volume levels when comparing options.

Listen and compare the spectrum

Noise colors do not all have the same physical definition. White, Pink and Brown have conventional idealized spectral forms, while Green, Soft White, Deep Brown, Warm Pink and Neutral Mid are variations whose actual measured profiles matter more than their names. Blue and Violet concentrate proportionally more energy at high frequencies.

Begin with the three standard reference colors, then compare one variation at a time using the dedicated page. Particularly bass-heavy masters may sound quiet on small speakers; especially bright ones can feel sharp. All sources are separate files, not a single sound renamed.

Measure the audio, not just the color name

Six independently produced sounds, six measured responses

Blue and Violet Noise correspond to established theoretical increasing-power spectra. Soft White, Deep Brown, Warm Pink and Neutral Mid are custom filtering labels, not universally agreed noise colors. These figures are fitted slopes from our approved 10-minute MP3 masters over the 120–6,000 Hz analysis interval, not claims of how loud they sound in a room.

Positive slopes emphasize higher frequencies; negative slopes shift energy lower, but speaker response, additional filtering and listening level matter.
