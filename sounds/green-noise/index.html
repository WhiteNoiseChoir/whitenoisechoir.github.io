How WhiteNoiseChoir Green Noise is made

We begin with newly generated Gaussian white noise and apply two smooth digital filters: a third-order high-pass at 165 Hz and a third-order low-pass at 1,550 Hz. The result emphasizes a broad middle region, approximately 250–1,000 Hz, without claiming that these cutoffs define every version of Green Noise.

This is a custom audio design, not a medical frequency or a standardized color. The filters reduce low rumble and high hiss; they do not literally recreate a forest, river or wind recording.

Green Noise questions

What frequency is Green Noise?

There is no universally standardized frequency for “Green Noise.” Our implementation uses a third-order 165 Hz high-pass and a third-order 1,550 Hz low-pass, so the result emphasizes the middle of the audible spectrum. Other producers may use different filters.

Is Green Noise the same as nature sounds?

No. Our track is synthetic filtered noise. It may sound softer or somewhat nature-like to some listeners, but it is not a forest, ocean, rain or wind field recording.

Can Green Noise help people sleep?

Some people prefer a stable mid-frequency sound while winding down; there is no clinically established benefit specific to this Green Noise design. Keep playback at a comfortable volume.

What makes this recording distinct from Pink Noise?

Pink Noise follows a power distribution near 1/f across a broad range; our Green Noise uses two filters to reduce both low bass and high treble. Try the live comparison above to hear the difference.

Will the recording stop at the 10-minute mark?

No. The 10-minute master repeats in the shared player, including native HLS where supported. An operating-system interruption or timer can still stop playback.
